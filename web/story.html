The case study

The night Bengaluru's lakes overflowed

How a flood on the city's tech corridor became the test for a climate-risk model built for property lenders. Scroll to follow the map.

4–5 September 2022

A night of torrential rain

After heavy rain on the night of 4 September 2022, large parts of Bengaluru went under water. Offices told staff to work from home and traffic in the east of the city came to a standstill.

The city has no major river. What flooded it was its own network of lakes.

Reported by Reuters and Deccan Herald.

The setting

A city built on a chain of lakes

Bengaluru sits on a plateau. Rain runs down shallow valleys through a chain of lakes, linked by stormwater drains. When one lake fills, it spills into the drain below and on to the next.

Low land beside the lakes and drains floods first. That pattern is what the flood model maps.

The tech corridor

Bellandur and the Outer Ring Road

Six overflowing lakes in the east and south-east flooded at least 75 places and nearly 4,000 homes. Among the worst hit were the Outer Ring Road near Marathahalli, Bellandur and Sarjapur Road.

This is where much of the city's office space sits, and where banks hold large property loans.

Downstream

Varthur, at the end of the chain

Water from Bellandur flows on towards Varthur. The model flags the low ground along this route as High flood susceptibility, from terrain, distance to lakes and drains, and how much water gathers there.

It did this without being told where the 2022 flood happened.

5 September 2022, from orbit

What the radar saw

The Sentinel-1 satellite passed over at the flood peak. Calm water looks dark to radar, so standing water shows up clearly in open ground.

Floodwater between buildings is harder: walls bounce the signal back, so the radar caught only the open-water part of the flood. Green cells are where it saw water inside a predicted High or Medium zone.
